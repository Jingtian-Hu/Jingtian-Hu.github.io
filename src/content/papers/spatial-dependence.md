The distribution of armed conflict exhibits spatial dependence: conflict has a high probability of spreading to neighboring areas. Yet existing conflict research, and conflict forecasting in particular, has not fully exploited this feature, and has largely ignored how the drivers of conflict interact at finer spatial scales. This has held back the development of more fine-grained forecasting. Advances in theories of spatial dependence, together with new spatial conflict data and spatial-analysis methods, now make it possible to bring theory and evidence together and put spatial dependence to work for prediction.

This article reviews the general approaches to modeling spatial dependence and sets out one way to capture it at finer spatiotemporal units. Using the case of Myanmar's internal conflict (2010–2020) within a machine-learning framework, and combining a split-population duration model with ensemble Bayesian model averaging, we show that taking spatial dependence seriously further improves forecast accuracy at finer spatiotemporal resolution. Even with only a small number of time-varying covariates, models built around the spatial dependence of the outcome itself, paired with appropriate machine-learning methods, achieve very high predictive accuracy. The approach has clear implications for building early-warning systems for armed conflict in Belt and Road partner countries.
